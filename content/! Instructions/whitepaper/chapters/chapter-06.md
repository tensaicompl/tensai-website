## 6.1 The Deflationary Paradox -- Why Cheaper AI Costs More

The cost of running an LLM at a fixed level of performance halves every two months (Epoch AI, 2025). No technology input in the history of computing has deflated this fast -- not storage, not bandwidth, not transistors.

GPT-4 launched in March 2023 at $30 per million input tokens and $60 per million output tokens. By late 2023, GPT-4 Turbo cut that to $8--15 per million output. GPT-4o arrived in mid-2024 at half the Turbo price. By January 2026, GPT-4o sat at $2.50 input and $10 output per million tokens -- a 92% reduction on input, 83% on output, in under three years (TokenMix, 2026). Google's Gemini 3.1 Flash reached $0.10 input and $0.40 output per million tokens -- a 99.7% price reduction from GPT-4's launch price. Models that cost $30 per million tokens in 2023 are meaningfully less capable than models that cost $0.10 per million tokens in 2026.

[VIS-36: LLM API Price Deflation Timeline]

The rate of decline varies by benchmark, but the direction is uniform: Epoch AI measured declines ranging from 9x to 900x per year across six performance benchmarks, with the fastest trends beginning after January 2024 (Epoch AI, 2025). Semiconductor providers are delivering 60--70% cost-per-token reductions per year on the inference hardware side alone (Goldman Sachs, 2026). The deflationary pressure is structural -- mixture-of-experts architectures activate only a fraction of parameters per token, driving inference costs down without sacrificing quality. The floor keeps dropping.

And yet enterprise AI spending surged 320% over the same period. Per-token costs dropped 1,000x in three years. Total spending increased 15x (Markus, 2025). Worldwide AI spending hit $2.59 trillion in 2026, up 47% from $1.76 trillion in 2025 (Gartner, 2026). Infrastructure alone accounts for $1.43 trillion -- 55% of total -- with AI-optimised servers expected to triple over five years. The big five hyperscalers are spending over $600 billion on infrastructure in 2026, roughly 75% of it AI-related, a 36% increase over 2025 (Friedman, 2026).

This is the Jevons paradox, first observed in 1865 when William Stanley Jevons noted that James Watt's more efficient steam engine did not reduce coal consumption -- it increased it, because efficiency made steam power economically viable for applications that were previously too expensive. The same mechanism is operating on tokens. Falling per-unit costs remove the financial gatekeeping that limited AI deployment. Every price reduction makes a new class of use case economically viable. The use cases expand. The tokens multiply. The bill grows.

[VIS-39: The Jevons Paradox Diagram]

The behavioural dimension makes it worse. TechCrunch coined the term "tokenmaxxing" in April 2026 to describe what was already happening across Silicon Valley: engineers optimising AI workflows for token consumption rather than business outcomes (TechCrunch, 2026). Google processes 3.2 quadrillion tokens per month (Tom's Hardware, 2026). Microsoft engineers admitted to deliberately inflating usage to avoid being seen as "using too little AI." Engineers with the largest token budgets produced the most pull requests -- but achieved only 2x throughput at 10x the token cost. One Anthropic developer ran up over $150,000 per month on Claude Code (TechCrunch, 2026). Uber burned through its entire 2026 AI coding tools budget in four months (Fortune, 2026). Microsoft began cancelling most direct Claude Code licences, moving engineers toward GitHub Copilot CLI to control costs.

[VIS-41: "One developer ran $150K/month on Claude Code."]

The agentic layer compounds the paradox exponentially. Agentic AI uses 1,000x more tokens than querying an LLM directly (Fortune, 2026). A single agent workflow that plans, reasons, uses tools, and iterates can consume in one task what a chatbot consumes in a thousand. Goldman Sachs projects token consumption will multiply 24x by 2030, reaching 120 quadrillion tokens per month, with 30% of queries being agentic. Beyond 2030, consumption could reach 55x current levels by 2040, with enterprise agents accounting for 70% or more of all token usage (Goldman Sachs, 2026).

EuroCorp's CFO looked at the API pricing page and concluded that AI was getting cheaper. He was right -- per token. He was wrong about the bill. The company's inference workloads now account for 55% of its AI infrastructure spending, up from 33% two years ago, because every cost reduction triggered a new batch of use cases. The demand-forecasting pilot that ran 500 queries per day in the sandbox runs 50,000 in production across nine countries. The customer-service chatbot that handled Tier 1 tickets now feeds an agent workflow that handles Tier 2 escalations, warranty lookups, and replacement shipments. Each expansion was individually rational. The aggregate was a budget that tripled while the unit cost fell by an order of magnitude.

By the end of 2025, the median enterprise was running AI inference across dozens of distinct use cases, with inference workloads accounting for 55% of AI infrastructure spending, up from 33% in 2023 (Markus, 2025). Each new use case was justified individually. The aggregate was never modelled.

Cost optimisation alone will not reduce your AI bill. It will make your AI bill grow more slowly than it otherwise would -- which is valuable, but it is not the same thing. The organisations that control AI spending are not the ones chasing cheaper tokens. They are the ones that can answer a different question: what does this token buy us?

## 6.2 Total Cost of Ownership -- The Hidden Multiplier

The ratio of what you actually spend on AI to what you budgeted for the model and its API runs between 2.3x and 4.1x (Ptolemay, 2025). The costs you see -- the model licence, the API calls, the cloud compute -- represent roughly a quarter of what you will actually spend. The rest is invisible until the invoices arrive.

68% of enterprise teams underestimate first-year LLM API spend by more than 3x (Ptolemay, 2025). Not by 10%. Not by 30%. By a factor of three. The underestimation is not incompetence -- it is structural. Pilot economics are fiction. A pilot runs a handful of queries per day against a subsidised API with a single user group and no monitoring overhead. Production runs thousands or millions of queries per day against infrastructure that must be reliable, observable, secure, and governed. Each of those adjectives adds cost that did not exist in the pilot budget.

The hidden cost categories compound in layers. Annual maintenance runs 15--30% of original build cost, covering monitoring, retraining, infrastructure management, and the steady accumulation of fixes (Xenoss, 2025). Integration complexity -- connecting the AI system to production data sources, authentication, existing workflows, and downstream consumers -- increases annual operational costs by 25--40% (Riseup Labs, 2026). Industries with real-time requirements face an additional 25--40% infrastructure premium. Change management -- the training, workflow redesign, and organisational adaptation required to make humans actually use and trust the system -- exceeds technical investment by a ratio of 3:1. Continuous model retraining consumes 22% more resources than initial deployment.

[VIS-37: AI TCO Calculator Template]

The pilot-to-production cost jump is where the fiction collapses. An e-commerce recommendation engine that cost $50,000 annually as a pilot cost $500,000 annually in production -- a 10x jump that nobody had modelled because the business case was built on pilot economics (Riseup Labs, 2026). The gap between entry-level and enterprise-scale self-hosted deployments illustrates the range: a 7B--13B parameter model on a single GPU runs $600--$3,000 per month. A mid-tier 30B--70B model setup with 4--8 GPUs runs $15,000--$40,000 per month. Enterprise scale -- 70B--100B+ models with high concurrency -- runs $50,000--$150,000 per month (Ptolemay, 2025). And infrastructure is only the beginning. A single H100 GPU cluster at $3 million represents just 35% of five-year TCO once you add the engineers ($150,000--$250,000 per AI infrastructure specialist), the hardware maintenance (15--25% of purchase price annually), and the operational overhead that keeps it all running.

84% of respondents in enterprise surveys report that AI costs eroded gross margins by more than 6%, with 25% or more seeing hits of 16% or higher (Xenoss, 2025). 85% of organisations misestimate AI project costs by more than 10% (Castle Rock Digital, 2025). The misestimation is not random -- it is consistently in one direction. Organisations underestimate because they budget for Day 1 and forget Day 2. Day 1 is the launch, the deployment, the press release. Day 2 is the continuous monitoring, retraining, drift detection, and incident response that a production system demands for its entire operational life. Day 2 is longer, more resource-intensive, and more expensive than Day 1. It is also the phase that most teams underinvest in, because the budget was built around a different day.

EuroCorp budgeted $4.2 million for its AI programme based on direct costs: model licensing, API calls, cloud compute, and a share of the AI team's salaries. The actual cost -- once data engineering, integration work, change management, monitoring infrastructure, security reviews, compliance documentation, and the opportunity cost of 14 engineers maintaining 23 pilots instead of building new capability were accounted for -- was closer to $9 million. The CFO saw $4.2 million. The organisation spent $9 million. The TCO multiplier was 2.1x on the visible budget, which falls at the low end of the 2.3--4.1x range -- only because EuroCorp had so few systems in production that the hidden costs had not fully compounded. Three production systems, twenty stalled pilots, and nobody could reconcile the two numbers because the cost attribution model did not exist.

The build-versus-buy decision collapses under TCO analysis for all but the highest-volume workloads. The breakeven threshold for self-hosting sits at approximately 11 billion tokens per month (Braincuber, 2026). Below that -- which encompasses the vast majority of enterprise deployments -- API-based cloud services win when you factor in idle GPU time, DevOps overhead, and engineering hours. At 500 million tokens per day, self-hosting a Llama 70B model costs roughly $4,360 per month versus $22,500 on API -- a 5x win that looks compelling until you calculate the engineering team required to keep it running. If GPU utilisation drops to 10%, the effective cost per 1,000 tokens jumps from $0.013 to $0.13 -- more expensive than premium APIs (Alpacked, 2026). API-based services win for 87% of use cases (Braincuber, 2026). The 13% where self-hosting wins are high-volume, high-concurrency workloads with predictable traffic patterns -- exactly the profile that most enterprises do not have during their first three years of AI deployment.

The TCO model is not a budgeting exercise. It is a governance instrument. If you cannot see the full cost, you cannot make the decision that matters: is this system worth running?

## 6.3 AI Unit Economics -- The Margin Compression Reality

Traditional SaaS enjoys gross margins of 80--90%. Each additional user costs almost nothing to serve. Marginal cost approaches zero. AI breaks this model structurally, not cyclically -- and the break is permanent.

Andreessen Horowitz made the argument in 2020 and the data has confirmed it every year since: AI companies have gross margins in the 50--60% range, well below the SaaS benchmark (a16z, 2020). Each user action triggers computationally intensive models. Marginal cost does not approach zero -- it approaches a floor set by inference cost, and that floor moves based on model complexity, input length, and output quality requirements. Maintaining AI models requires significant customer-specific work -- fine-tuning, prompt engineering, evaluation, edge case handling. AI companies combine elements of software and services. This is a fundamentally different economic model.

ICONIQ Capital's January 2026 snapshot puts the average AI product gross margin at 52%, up from 41% in 2024 and 45% in 2025 -- improving, but still 28 points below pre-AI SaaS benchmarks (ICONIQ, 2026). LLM-native companies maintain roughly 65% gross margin while growing around 400% year-on-year (Bessemer, 2025). Inference averages 23% of total revenue at scaling-stage AI B2B companies (ICONIQ, 2026). The structural arithmetic is straightforward: pre-AI 80% gross margins minus 12--17 percentage points of AI compute and operations equals post-AI margins of 63--68% -- and that is for mature operators who have optimised aggressively.

The compression flows downstream to enterprise buyers through what Tropic calls the "AI tax" -- software vendors imposing AI-driven price increases of 20--37% on enterprise renewals, delivered through forced SKU migrations and credit-based pricing (Tropic, 2026). Buyers who negotiate reduce vendor asks by 55%, which sounds like a win until you realise that final pricing still lands 12% above pre-AI baselines even after negotiation. The margin compression that AI creates for software vendors is being passed through to their customers. You are paying for it, whether you use the AI features or not.

92% of AI software companies now use mixed pricing models -- subscription plus usage fees -- because pure subscription pricing cannot absorb variable inference costs (ICONIQ, 2026). This pricing shift transfers cost uncertainty from the vendor to the buyer. Under subscription pricing, you know your annual software bill in advance. Under mixed pricing, your bill fluctuates with usage -- and AI usage, as Section 6.1 established, tends to grow faster than anyone projects.

The long tail makes the margin problem worse. a16z's follow-up analysis documented what they called "taming the tail": AI products need high accuracy even in rare situations, and while any given edge case may be rare, there are many rare situations in aggregate (a16z, 2020). Addressing the tail -- the unusual queries, the complex edge cases, the inputs the model has not seen -- drives cost and complexity disproportionately. The first 90% of accuracy is cheap. The last 10% is where the economics break.

The spending data confirms the structural shift. Companies spent $37 billion on generative AI in 2025, up from $11.5 billion in 2024 -- a 3.2x year-on-year increase (Menlo Ventures, 2025). Inference-related costs now account for 4--9% of revenue at public SaaS companies. AI accounts for 22% of total cloud spend for traditional SaaS, up from near zero 18 months ago. AI companies typically spend 25% or more of revenue on cloud infrastructure (a16z, 2020). The economics of software delivery have shifted permanently, and the vendors passing those costs to buyers through the AI tax are simply acknowledging a reality that their income statements already reflect.

For EuroCorp, the margin compression has a specific operational consequence. The three AI systems in production are not free to run -- they carry ongoing inference costs that scale with usage, maintenance costs that scale with complexity, and vendor costs that increase annually regardless of value delivered. The CFO's question from Chapter 1 -- "which of these is generating measurable return?" -- is not just a question about revenue impact. It is a question about whether the unit economics of each system close. A system that saves $200,000 per year but costs $180,000 to operate is not a success. It is a system with a 10% margin that one price increase or one usage spike will push negative.

## 6.4 The Optimisation Stack -- Levers Ranked by Impact

Six cost optimisation levers exist, ranked here by impact and implementation speed. The order matters -- implementing them in the wrong sequence wastes effort on low-impact changes while leaving the highest-impact opportunities untouched.

[VIS-38: Optimisation Stack Roadmap]

**Lever 1: Prompt caching (90% savings on cached input -- Week 1).** The single highest-impact cost lever for API-heavy workloads, and the one most organisations implement last when they should implement it first. Anthropic's prompt caching prices cached input tokens at 0.1x base price -- a 90% discount. Claude Opus 4.7: $5.00 per million input tokens standard, $0.50 cached. Claude Sonnet 4.6: $3.00 standard, $0.30 cached. Combining prompt caching with batch API processing can reduce costs by up to 95% (Anthropic, 2026). A customer support chatbot with a 20,000-token knowledge base drops from $0.06 per conversation to $0.006 with caching. At 10,000 conversations per day, that is $540 saved daily -- $197,000 annually -- from a configuration change that takes hours to implement (PromptBuilder, 2025). One practitioner reported API costs dropping from $720 per month to $72 with prompt caching alone (Medium, 2025). 31% of LLM queries exhibit semantic similarity, meaning nearly a third of enterprise API spend is wasted on re-processing inputs the system has already seen.

**Lever 2: Model routing and tiering (30--70% cost reduction -- Weeks 2--4).** 50--70% of enterprise LLM requests can be handled by the cheapest model tier. Only 5--15% require the most expensive (MindStudio, 2026). A well-designed router classifies requests by complexity and routes them to the appropriate tier. The pricing spread across tiers in early 2026: premium models (Opus-class) at $30--60 per million tokens, mid-tier (Sonnet-class) at $10--15, lightweight (Haiku-class) at $0.50--2, small open-source models (Llama, Mistral) at $0.10--$0.50, and local inference at fractions of a cent. Organisations using routers report 30--70% cost reductions while maintaining quality benchmarks. Roughly 85% of enterprise queries can be handled by budget-tier models (AICC, 2025). The routing question is not "which model is best?" -- it is "what is the cheapest model that is good enough for this specific request?"

**Lever 3: Semantic caching (40--80% for repetitive workloads -- Month 1).** Semantic caching identifies queries that are similar in meaning, not just identical in text, and returns cached responses. AWS research on 63,796 real chatbot queries demonstrated 86% cost reduction and 88% latency improvement on cached responses, with cache hit rates above 90% maintaining 91% response accuracy (VentureBeat, 2026). Response times improve from 850ms to under 120ms for cached queries. Customer-facing applications achieve 25--40% cache hit rates; internal tools with repetitive queries reach 50--60% (Percona, 2025). AWS Bedrock, Azure OpenAI Service, and Google Cloud Vertex AI now offer native semantic caching -- this has moved from experimental to production infrastructure.

**Lever 4: Batch processing (50% discount -- Month 1).** Most enterprise AI workloads are not real-time. Document processing, report generation, data analysis, and content creation can tolerate latency in exchange for significant cost reduction. Anthropic and OpenAI both offer batch processing at roughly 50% of on-demand pricing. The implementation is straightforward: queue non-urgent requests, process them in bulk during off-peak hours, deliver results asynchronously. The constraint is organisational, not technical -- teams default to synchronous processing because the pilot was built that way.

**Lever 5: Prompt compression (15--40% savings -- Month 1).** Prompt compression reduces token count while preserving semantic information. Shorter prompts cost less. The savings are modest compared to caching and routing, but they compound across high-volume workloads and require no infrastructure changes -- just better prompt engineering. The discipline of writing shorter, more precise prompts also tends to improve output quality, making this the only optimisation lever that simultaneously reduces cost and increases performance.

**Lever 6: Model distillation (50--85% long-term -- Quarter 1--2).** Training a smaller, specialised model to replicate a larger model's behaviour on a specific task. The smaller model runs faster and cheaper while maintaining comparable quality on the narrow domain it was trained for. Distillation achieves 50--85% cost reductions but requires upfront investment in training data, evaluation pipelines, and ML engineering expertise (Morph LLM, 2025). It is the highest-impact lever for mature, high-volume, well-understood workloads -- and the wrong lever for anything still in experimentation.

The implementation sequence matters because it follows an effort-impact curve. Prompt caching and model routing can be implemented in the first month with immediate, measurable impact. Semantic caching and batch processing require modest infrastructure changes but pay back within weeks. Prompt compression is ongoing discipline. Distillation is a quarterly engineering investment. An enterprise that starts with distillation and ignores caching is spending months on a 50--85% reduction when it could have captured a 90% reduction in days.

## 6.5 From Tokens to Outcomes -- The AI FinOps Maturity Model

Cost-per-token is a vanity metric. It tells you how much you spend on the commodity input. It tells you nothing about whether the commodity input produced anything worth having.

98% of FinOps teams now manage AI spend, up from 63% in 2025 and 31% in 2024 (FinOps Foundation, 2026). The acceleration is not enthusiasm -- it is panic. Average monthly AI spend per organisation hit $85,521 in 2025, up 36% from $62,964 the year before (CloudZero, 2025). 45% of organisations spend over $100,000 per month, doubled from 20% in 2024. Enterprise LLM API spending doubled in six months, from $3.5 billion in late 2024 to $8.4 billion by mid-2025 (AICC, 2025). The spend is growing faster than most organisations' ability to track it.

[VIS-40: AI Cost Attribution Model]

Only 51% of organisations can confidently evaluate the ROI of their AI costs (CloudZero, 2025). The other 49% are spending -- often six figures monthly -- without knowing whether the expenditure generates value. More than half of finance executives cannot clearly demonstrate ROI from AI investments (Larridin, 2025). This is not a measurement problem. It is a governance failure dressed as a data gap.

The FinOps Foundation's Crawl-Walk-Run maturity model, adapted for AI, maps the progression that enterprises must navigate (FinOps Foundation, 2026). **Crawl** is experimentation: fail fast, learn what works, do not worry about unit economics because the volumes are small enough that waste does not matter. **Walk** is validated solutions: separate AI budgets from general cloud spend, establish cost attribution by team and use case, begin tracking cost per outcome rather than cost per token. **Run** is core business: anomaly tracking, benchmarking against industry peers, continuous optimisation, and AI costs governed with the same rigour as headcount or capital expenditure.

The unit economics evolution follows a parallel trajectory: cost per token gives way to cost per assist, which gives way to cost per agent action, which gives way to cost per case deflected or cost per outcome achieved (FinOps Foundation, 2026). Each step in that progression represents a move from measuring what the system consumes to measuring what the system produces. The first is an infrastructure metric. The last is a business metric. Most enterprises are stuck at the first.

The attribution challenge is specific to AI and absent from traditional cloud FinOps. LLM API calls have no resource to tag -- they are transactions, not assets. There is no server instance you can label with a cost centre. There is no storage volume you can attribute to a department. The metadata required for attribution -- which team, which product, which feature, which use case generated this API call -- must be captured at the application layer and propagated through the billing pipeline. Chargeback models designed for infrastructure create chaos when applied to AI spend without modification (FinOps Foundation, 2026). AI costs can vary by an order of magnitude week-to-week based on feature adoption, prompt changes, or model upgrades. A quarterly budget review cadence that works for server costs is dangerously slow for AI costs.

The FinOps Foundation recommends ten KPIs for AI cost management: cost per inference, training cost efficiency, token consumption metrics, resource utilisation, ROI per use case, cost per API call, time to business value, time to first prompt, model-quality alignment score, and budget variance (FinOps Foundation, 2026). The list is useful. The principle behind it is more useful: match model complexity to actual business requirements. The most expensive model is not the best model for every task. The cheapest model is not the best model for any task. The right model is the one whose cost-quality tradeoff aligns with the value of the outcome it produces.

EuroCorp's AI programme spent $380,000 per month with no cost attribution by use case, no tracking of cost per outcome, and no mechanism to distinguish between AI spend that generated value and AI spend that generated heat. The CFO could see the aggregate bill. He could not see which of the 23 initiatives -- including the 20 in limbo -- were consuming what share of it. The three production systems might have been profitable. The 20 stalled pilots might have been consuming more than the three combined. Nobody knew, because the organisation had never built the instrumentation to answer the question.

McKinsey frames the budget tension cleanly: companies must allocate between "run" expenditures -- keeping existing systems operating -- and "change" expenditures -- building new capabilities. AI is compounding this challenge because it transforms entire operating models while simultaneously consuming up to a third of companies' change budgets (McKinsey, 2025). Technology leaders must decide allocation in tandem with the C-suite, requiring alignment between technology and business objectives that most organisations have not achieved. The AI budget is not a line item. It is a structural decision about how much of the organisation's capacity for change gets consumed by keeping AI systems alive versus building new ones.

The transition from tokens to outcomes is not a reporting exercise. It is a cost governance architecture -- tagging at the application layer, attribution pipelines, chargeback models adapted for AI, and review cadences fast enough to catch anomalies before they compound. Without it, you are flying a $380,000-per-month programme on instruments that show speed and altitude but not fuel consumption or destination.

## 6.6 Measuring What Matters -- AI ROI Beyond Time Saved

"Time saved" is the most common AI metric and the least useful one. It measures the input the system displaces, not the output the system creates.

Bain's research at the task level exposes the gap: developers using AI complete 21% more tasks and merge 98% more pull requests, yet company-wide delivery metrics show no measurable organisational impact (Bain, 2025). HBR calls this the "micro-productivity trap" -- task-level gains that do not translate to firm-level value (HBR, 2026). 37% of time saved by AI is offset by rework (McKinsey, 2025). The AI makes the task faster. The rework makes the outcome no better. Net impact: zero, dressed in impressive task-level metrics.

The Tropic "Double-Click Framework" cuts through this: when someone says "time saved," ask "so what?" -- and keep asking until you reach a business outcome you can actually measure (Tropic, 2026). A customer-service chatbot saves agents 30 minutes per day. So what? That time is reallocated to complex cases. So what? Complex case resolution improves by 15%. So what? Customer retention in the segment those cases serve increases by 3 percentage points. So what? That retention increase is worth $2.4 million annually. The time saved is not the ROI. The retention increase is the ROI. Everything between the first metric and the last is connective tissue that most organisations never build.

The "Team Output Framework" offers a parallel lens: compare team output against team size over time (Tropic, 2026). If output rises while headcount stays flat, AI is working at the organisational level -- not just the task level. This is the only metric that resolves the micro-productivity trap, because it measures what the organisation produces rather than what the tool accelerates.

BCG's 10-20-70 principle reframes where ROI actually lives: 10% of AI value comes from algorithms, 20% from data and technology, 70% from developing new business processes or transforming business functions (BCG, 2025). An automotive manufacturer used AI predictive maintenance across 47 facilities and achieved a 41% reduction in unplanned downtime, a 36% decrease in maintenance costs, and $127 million in annual savings. A global retailer deployed AI demand forecasting across 2,800 stores and achieved a 28% reduction in excess inventory with a 34% improvement in stock availability. The value did not come from the model. It came from the process redesign the model enabled. Cross-functional optimisation delivers 45% greater savings than department-specific initiatives (BCG, 2025).

BCG's "depth over breadth" finding is the counterintuitive corollary: leaders average 3.5 use cases versus 6.1 for their peers, yet achieve 2.1x greater ROI (BCG, 2025). Fewer use cases, done properly, with process redesign, change management, and governance built in, outperform broad experimentation across many use cases done superficially. 70% of AI value concentrates in five functions: sales, marketing, supply chain, manufacturing, and pricing (BCG, 2025). The highest-returning AI investments are deep, focused, and organisationally embedded -- not broad, experimental, and technologically interesting.

McKinsey's data underscores the structural nature of the disconnect. Almost 80% of companies reported using generative AI in 2025. About the same proportion reported that the tools had not significantly affected their earnings (McKinsey, 2025). The imbalance between horizontal copilots -- tools that assist broadly but shallowly -- and transformative vertical use cases explains the gap. Roughly 90% of transformative vertical use cases remain stuck in pilot mode. The copilots that reached production are the ones easiest to deploy and hardest to measure. The vertical applications that would move earnings are the ones hardest to deploy and easiest to measure. The irony is precise: organisations can measure the ROI of the use cases they cannot build, and cannot measure the ROI of the use cases they have deployed.

Deloitte's ROI data provides the timeline context: only 6% of organisations report payback in under a year. Among the most successful projects, just 13% see returns within 12 months. Most report satisfactory ROI within 2--4 years (Deloitte, 2025). This timeline -- significantly longer than the typical 7--12 month expectation for technology investments -- is the gap that kills executive sponsorship. A CFO who expects AI to pay back in a year and sees no return after 12 months will cut the budget. The programme needed 30 months. It got 12. The failure mode is not the technology -- it is the mismatch between AI's return horizon and the organisation's budget cycle.

ROI measurement for AI requires four dimensions, not one. **Financial**: cost per inference, cost per outcome, margin impact, budget variance. **Operational**: workflow cycle time reduction, error rate change, throughput increase, automation rate, model reliability. **Strategic**: new capabilities enabled, time-to-market acceleration, competitive positioning, innovation velocity. **Human**: employee satisfaction with AI tools, skill development velocity, adoption depth -- not breadth -- and retention impact. "Time saved" appears nowhere on this list, because it is an intermediate input, not a terminal output. Measuring AI by time saved is like measuring a factory by how fast the conveyor belt moves rather than how many finished products ship.

UC Berkeley's professional education programme argued in 2025 that traditional ROI may be the wrong primary metric for many AI investments altogether (UC Berkeley, 2025). AI creates optionality and capability that standard ROI cannot capture -- the ability to enter new markets, to respond to competitive threats faster, to offer products that were previously infeasible. The optionality value is real but resists quantification, which means it resists budget allocation committees. The four-dimension scorecard does not solve this tension -- but it makes the invisible dimensions visible, which is the prerequisite for funding them.

Companies with structured upskilling programmes see 2x AI ROI -- 42% versus 21% for those without (McKinsey, 2025). The human dimension of the scorecard is not a soft metric. It is a predictor of financial performance. Organisations that invest in adoption depth -- ensuring that the humans using AI tools are skilled enough to extract value from them -- generate twice the return of organisations that deploy the same tools without the investment. The technology is a constant. The human variable is the multiplier.

## 6.7 The Agent Economics Problem

Everything in Sections 6.1 through 6.6 describes the economics of AI as a tool you query. Agentic AI -- systems that plan, reason, use tools, and operate with bounded autonomy -- changes every variable in the cost equation, and changes most of them for the worse.

Token consumption per task jumped 10x--100x since December 2023, driven by multi-step agentic processes where each reasoning step, tool call, and iteration consumes tokens (DataRobot, 2026). A chatbot query uses tens of thousands of tokens. An agent workflow that researches, plans, executes, evaluates, and iterates on the same task can use millions. The difference between smart and sloppy agent deployment is a factor of 10 in operating cost (DataRobot, 2026). Optimising for accuracy alone -- building agents that try every approach and iterate until the answer is perfect -- yields agents 4.4--10.8x more expensive than cost-aware alternatives that achieve comparable performance (Galileo, 2026).

Without controls, an AI agent can cost more than the employee it was designed to augment (CIO.com, 2026). Running a persistent agent against a frontier model API with high token consumption pushes cost per task above human worker cost. A tightly scoped agent with a smaller, task-specific model costs significantly less -- but building the scoping and routing requires the cost governance infrastructure described in Sections 6.4 and 6.5. The agent is only cheaper than the human when the agent is designed to be cheaper than the human. Left undesigned, it is not.

The cost benchmarks are emerging but divergent. Customer service AI resolutions average $0.62 per interaction versus $7.40 for human agents -- a compelling 12:1 ratio (Digital Applied, 2026). Chat-specific interactions run $0.41. Voice-AI runs $1.18. But these figures describe simple, well-scoped, single-step interactions against optimised models. Enterprise agent deployments -- multi-step, multi-tool, requiring compliance, audit trails, and governance overhead -- run significantly higher. A coding assistant integration costs roughly $500,000 for a single organisation: six engineers, three to four months (Codebridge, 2026). A general customer service chatbot with custom integrations runs approximately $2 million: eight engineers, nine months. AI agent development ranges from $15,000 for a simple single-task agent to $400,000 or more for enterprise multi-agent systems with compliance requirements. Most mid-market implementations fall in the $40,000--$150,000 range.

40% of agentic AI projects fail before reaching production (DataRobot, 2026). First-year TCO typically runs 40--80% higher than initial build cost. These are not pilot-era economics -- these are the numbers from organisations that made it past the pilot, invested in production infrastructure, and still found the costs exceeding projections. The agentic AI cost problem is not that agents are expensive. It is that agents are unpredictably expensive, because their token consumption varies with task complexity in ways that are difficult to forecast and impossible to cap without governance mechanisms that most organisations have not built.

Goldman Sachs projects AI queries will jump from 5 billion in 2025 to 23 billion in 2030, with enterprise agents accounting for 70% or more of all token usage by 2040 (Goldman Sachs, 2026). Gartner projects that agentic AI could drive 30% of enterprise software revenue by 2035 -- more than $450 billion (Gartner, 2025). The economics of agents will dominate the economics of enterprise AI within five years. Organisations that build cost governance for today's chatbots but not tomorrow's agents are building a levee for last year's flood.

The agent cost governance model requires four mechanisms that most enterprises lack. **Budget caps**: hard ceilings on token expenditure per agent per task, with automatic fallback to human processing when the ceiling is reached. **Usage tiers**: routing within the agent workflow itself, using frontier models for reasoning and planning steps and lightweight models for data retrieval and formatting. **Outcome-based budgeting**: defining the acceptable cost per successful task before deployment, not after the first invoice arrives, and killing agents whose cost per success exceeds the value of the success. **Cost-aware agent design**: building cost constraints into the agent's objective function so that the system optimises for the best answer at an acceptable cost, not the best answer at any cost.

EuroCorp's logistics division is piloting an agent that handles carrier rate negotiations. The aftermarket services team is testing an agent that triages warranty claims and initiates replacement shipments. Neither pilot has a cost model. Neither tracks cost per successful negotiation or cost per claim resolved. Neither has defined a budget cap, a fallback to human processing when costs exceed thresholds, or a mechanism to compare agent cost against the human cost the agent is supposed to displace. The agents will likely work -- the technology is capable. The question is not whether they work but whether they work at a cost that justifies running them. That question requires the cost attribution, unit economics, and outcome measurement infrastructure described in this chapter. Without it, EuroCorp will add two more lines to its $380,000 monthly bill and still not know whether any of it is generating value.

---

The economics described in this chapter determine what is sustainable. Sustainable AI is AI whose costs are visible, attributable, and justified by the outcomes it produces. Every other kind -- the AI that runs on faith, the AI that hides behind vanity metrics, the AI whose costs are buried in a general cloud budget that nobody examines -- is AI that will be cut when the CFO asks the question that EuroCorp's CFO asked in Chapter 1.

But sustainability is only half the equation. A system can be economically justified and still untrustworthy -- leaking data, vulnerable to prompt injection, operating without audit trails, making decisions that nobody can explain. Chapter 7 builds the security and trust architecture that determines whether the systems you can afford to run are systems you can afford to trust.
